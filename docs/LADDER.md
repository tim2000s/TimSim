# Which layers change the person, and which only change the run

`FIDELITY.md` reports where the fidelity signatures land as each layer is added. That table
has a weakness it does not state: it changes the driving algorithm partway up, running its
first three rungs open loop and its last three under the built-in correction loop. So a reader
cannot tell whether the sensor and site layers move those statistics because of what they do
to the simulated person or because of what a loop does in response.

This is the same ladder walked four times, once per driver, with the layers held constant
within each walk. Two of the four drivers are other people's shipped code called across a
pipe: Boost through a JVM and oref0 SMB through node. Neither knows this package exists, so
agreement between them is not something the package can arrange.

The question is not which driver produces better glucose. It is whether adding a layer moves
a signature the same way regardless of what is dosing. A layer that does is a property of the
person. A layer that does not is an interaction with the controller, and this package should
not describe it as physiology.

Twenty adult subjects, 28 days, seed 0, generated by
`examples/layer_ladder_by_controller.py`.

## Two things this ladder does differently

Behaviour comes before physiology. The first walk used the order `layer_ablation.py` uses and
lost half the cohort at the physiology rung: sensitivity that moves through the day and week
to week, with nobody yet able to treat a low, produces subjects who go low and are never
treated. Adding a mechanism before its antidote left only four of ten subjects completing
every arm. In this order thirteen of twenty do, and no rung loses more than three.

| Driver | stock 2008 | +behaviour | +physiology | +sensor | +site |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| basal-bolus | 20/20 | 20/20 | 20/20 | 20/20 | 18/20 |
| trend | 20/20 | 20/20 | 19/20 | 20/20 | 20/20 |
| boost | 19/20 | 19/20 | 20/20 | 19/20 | 20/20 |
| oref | 17/20 | 19/20 | 20/20 | 19/20 | 19/20 |

And the set contains a statistic that can see the physiology layer. `drift` cannot: it reads
the built-in controller's own learned correction factor, so it is undefined for any other
driver and reads 5.77 %CV with the person's weekly sensitivity variation switched off
entirely against 8.69 with it on. It is reported below and labelled as not evidence.
`insulin_cv`, the week-to-week variation in total daily insulin, replaces it. The reason no
glucose statistic could do the job is structural: a closed loop absorbs a sensitivity change
by altering delivery, which is exactly what keeps the glucose flat, so the signal moves out of
glucose and into insulin as the loop closes.

Each cell is the mean across drivers of that driver's median subject. The last column is the
spread between drivers, which is how much of the number belongs to what is dosing rather than

the 2.4 mg/dL of jitter that is quantisation alone. UVA/Padova cannot produce those numbers
because it contains nothing capable of producing them.

## Which movements are carried by the person

A movement counts when its paired confidence interval excludes zero. `person` means it does so
under all four drivers and points the same way each time.

Twelve of forty-seven survive, and every layer now carries something.

The behaviour layer carries five. Hypoglycaemia recovery shortens by 38.6 minutes under every
driver, taking it from 99.4 minutes into the real range of 50 to 59. Rescue carbohydrate is a
decision the person makes rather than anything an algorithm sees, which is why no controller
changes the answer.

The sensor layer carries three. Compression lows go from nothing to 3.47 per thirty days and
jitter from quantisation to 3.15 mg/dL above it, both into the real range and both under every
driver. Nothing a controller does can alter how a sensor reports, so this is what the design
predicts and it is worth having as a check on the method rather than as news.

The site layer carries three: glucose variability up 2.74 points, and both autocorrelations.
An absorption failure widens the distribution and makes the trace more predictable for a few
hours.

The physiology layer carries one, and this is the change from the previous walk. Diurnal
amplitude falls 10.0 mg/dL under every driver, from 67.2 to 59.7 against a real range of 34.7
to 56.3. At ten subjects the same movement appeared under two drivers of four and could not be
claimed; at twenty it appears under all four. The earlier negative result was a question of
power rather than a finding about the layer.

## What the physiology layer does not yet do

It does not move the statistic built to detect it. `insulin_cv` responds under one driver of
four, +0.89 under Boost, and the whole layer adds between 0.1 and 0.9 percentage points
depending on who is driving. Since stock sits at 1.8% and the full stack at 4.0%, against a
real median of 7.3% and an interquartile range of 4.5 to 11.1, these virtual people vary their
weekly insulin requirement by roughly half what real people do.

That is a calibration statement rather than a structural one, and it is the first quantitative
statement this package has been able to make about the layer. Whether to raise the term is a
decision that has not been taken here, because raising a parameter until a statistic matches
is fitting rather than validating, and the same statistic would then no longer be evidence for
anything.

oref showing the smallest response, +0.12, is worth recording without an explanation attached.
It is the one driver running sensitivity detection of its own and should have been the most
responsive. Its baseline variation is also the highest of the three closed loops, which would
hurt the signal-to-noise, but that has not been measured.

## Where the model is furthest from real people

Four signatures sit outside the real range with every layer on. Outcome spread at a stuck high
is 23.1 against 26.5 to 33.5, so the model is more predictable than a real person. Both
autocorrelations are above their ranges, which is the same thing said differently. Diurnal
amplitude is 67.6 against 34.7 to 56.3, and it carries the second largest driver spread in the
table, so part of it belongs to the algorithm.

Hypoglycaemia rebound is the largest single gap, 58.5 against 23.2 to 28.4. The behaviour
layer raises it 29.3 points under every driver, which makes it a property of the person as
modelled rather than an accident of one run. Overcorrecting a low is a real behaviour and the
reference range is real people doing it; the simulated people do it about twice as often.

The bare model is inside the range on this signature, at 26.7% and 23.3% under two drivers on
436 and 368 episodes, and it gets there by failing the other half of the same event: its
recoveries take 110 and 100 minutes against a real 50 to 59, and it spends 0.25% and 1.05% of
time below 70 against a real 4.07%. Lows that rare and that long end when the insulin clears,
with nothing absorbing to carry glucose past 180. The trade is therefore forced rather than
incidental, since the carbohydrate that brings recovery into range is what overshoots, and a
rebound rate conditioned on a quarter of the real exposure is not the same measurement anyway.

## Reproducing

    python examples/layer_ladder_by_controller.py --subjects 20 --days 28 --workers 8
    python examples/layer_ladder_by_controller.py --table

The two bridged drivers need `bridge/boost/build.sh` and `bridge/oref/build.sh` to have been
run, and each holds a subprocess per subject. The full twenty arms take about two and a half
hours on eight workers.
